What the platform does

CHemTrace shows how contaminants move through a Swiss landscape: arsenic (As), mercury (Hg) and lead (Pb) today, with more contaminants being added. It also gives screening estimates for a release at a real Swiss site. It has two modes:

ModeMade forWhat you doWhat you get
ExploreLearning, teaching, a first lookPoint at parts of an animated landscapeThe metal's forms, where it moves next, its threat to people, how to trap it
AdvancedResearchers and consultantsEnter a release and site data, or click a place on the mapConcentrations compared with Swiss limits, uncertainty ranges, measured Swiss benchmarks, recommended measures, a copyable report

Results are screening-level estimates. They do not replace a site investigation.

Choose a metal in the Metal menu at the top right and a mode with the Explore and Advanced buttons; the page colour follows the metal. The site remembers your metal, mode, theme and inputs in your browser.

Contact and feedback: Li-Ling T., ETH Zürich, litail@ethz.ch. More contaminants are being added; suggestions for which ones matter most to you are welcome.

Intended use and limits

CHemTrace gives an overview of the whole system: soil, air, groundwater, rivers and lakes together. Use it to form assumptions and a first plan of where to act and what to measure. The detail belongs in specialised models and site investigations; the platform hands its inputs to them (see Take it further). Version 0.13.0 is a research prototype: transparent and documented, but not yet reviewed by independent experts.

  1. Overview here. Follow the release through all compartments, see where most of it ends up and which result is close to a Swiss limit.
  2. First plan. Rank the places to act and the measurements that narrow the range most (spread map, mass budget, uncertainty drivers).
  3. Detail elsewhere. Check the top priority with site data and a specialised model: PHREEQC for chemistry, HYDRUS-1D for the soil profile, MODFLOW 6 for the plume.
Use it forUse it with care forDo not use it for
An overview of a site and its pathways; a first plan of where to act and what to measure; comparing scenarios; preparing input for specialised models; teaching and outreach Preliminary plausibility checks by consultants, next to measured data, with the version and inputs recorded (Copy inputs, Copy report) Deciding under the AltlV whether a site must be investigated further or remediated; remediation design; concentrations or arrival times at a specific well; individual health-risk assessments; expert or legal reports
  • Precision. Results are shown with two significant figures because they are often uncertain by a factor of 10 or more. Read the 90% range first.
  • Assumptions. Several defaults were set by the developer without a published source. The assumptions page lists every one, and says which need expert review.
  • Validation. The model matches 8 of 17 published Swiss benchmarks and 5 partly; arrival times in groundwater, antimony seepage and mercury loss from topsoil do not match. See the validation page.
  • Swiss law. Contaminated-site decisions rest on measured soil, eluate and groundwater data. Check limit values against the current text on fedlex.admin.ch.

Explore mode

Explore mode is a field guide. It shows a cross-section from glacier to lake; point at any part to learn what the metal does there.

The landscape. It contains atmosphere, glacier, bedrock and ore veins, forest soil, farmland and gardens, a contaminated site, village, industry, wastewater plant (ARA), river, lake, lake sediment and groundwater. The contaminated site changes with the metal: a mine dump for arsenic, a waste canal for mercury, a shooting-range berm for lead.

The moving dots show where the metal travels:

  • Filled circle: dissolved in water
  • Filled square: bound to particles
  • Ring: gas
  • Thicker line: larger flow (indicative only, not calculated)

The side panel. Hover over a part to preview it (tap on a phone). Click to pin it, and use the back button to return to the metal profile. For each part the panel shows:

  • mobility
  • the forms you will find
  • what happens there
  • what changes in the current scenario
  • where the metal moves next and where it comes from (both clickable)
  • the threat to people
  • where and how to trap it
  • a Swiss example
  • the model inputs that matter

Scenario switches change the chemistry: Redox (Oxic or Reducing), Soil and water pH (Calcareous or Acidic) and Water regime (Base flow, Snowmelt or Storm). After each switch, the flows that change light up for a few seconds, red where more metal moves and green where less, with the factor (for example ×5), and a note below the profile lists the largest changes. Pause stops the animation.

Try this: choose lead and switch Soil and water pH to Acidic: leaching from farmland to groundwater rises about five-fold. Then choose arsenic, set Redox to Reducing and hover over Groundwater. Then choose mercury and hover over the Lake to see methylation.

Pick a site from the map

One click on a place in Switzerland fills the site inputs from national datasets.

  1. In Advanced mode, click the map. You can zoom with + and − or the scroll wheel. You can also type LV95 coordinates (E and N) and click Look up.
  2. Read the site table. It lists:
    • elevation, precipitation and mean temperature
    • soil pH, clay and organic carbon
    • aquifer, bedrock and land use
    • natural background of As, Pb and Hg, plus classes for antimony, iron and calcium
    Notes appear for crystalline bedrock (natural arsenic possible), calcareous soil and karst.
  3. Click Fill inputs from this site. This sets precipitation, soil pH, organic carbon, iron oxides, background As, Pb and Hg, aquifer type with hydraulic conductivity and porosity, and land use.
  4. Change anything you know better from your own site data.

Good to know:

  • Values come from a 1 km grid. They describe the area, not a single parcel.
  • Built-up areas have no organic-carbon map, so that input keeps its previous value there.
  • Settlements are set to Garden or playground, which applies the strictest limits. Change it to Industrial if that fits.
  • To find LV95 coordinates for an address, right-click it on map.geo.admin.ch.

What else is on a card:

  • Uncertainty bar. The grey band is the 90% range from 400 model runs with varied inputs. The dot is the median and the red tick is the limit.
  • % of runs above is the chance of exceeding the limit, given the uncertainty.
  • Measured shows what was measured at comparable Swiss sites, so you can check the model.
  • The two small grey lines name the limit used and show how the number was calculated.

Around the cards:

  • On the landscape, badges (✓, ?, !) mark each affected part and a pulsing ring marks the release point.
  • The side panel shows a run summary with key results and first actions. Hover over a part of the landscape to see “Your scenario here”.
  • Where and how to trap it lists measures that follow from your results.
  • Copy report puts a text summary on your clipboard for notes or e-mail.
  • Screening equations and assumptions at the bottom of the results lists every formula and default.

Where to act: the spread map

Below the result cards, a map shows where the metal is above the Swiss standard at each point in time, so you can decide where to remove or cover soil, which wells and springs to protect, and where to monitor.

  1. Set the source. Pick your site on the site map and click Fill inputs from this site, or click Move source on the spread map and then click the place. Presets use an illustrative place.
  2. Set the groundwater flow direction. This matters most. Read it from your canton's groundwater map (water-table contours), the FOEN hydrogeological map 1:100,000 or your own wells. Then click Set direction and click a point downstream of the source, or type the direction in degrees on the Groundwater tab. Until you do, a dashed arrow from the terrain stands in and the page says so.
  3. Move the time slider (1–100 years) or press Play.
  4. Read the colours. Red: above the standard at the best estimate. Amber: at least 10% of the uncertainty runs are above it. The source area turns red or amber when its topsoil is above a Swiss soil value. The dashed amber line is the runoff path that eroded soil follows; a red dot marks where it reaches a stream, an open circle where it collects without one. The green arrow is the groundwater direction: solid if you set it, dashed for the terrain stand-in. The blue dotted arrow is the wind.
  5. Read "Where to act" below the map. It gives the soil concentration at the source, the mass washed downslope, and how far and wide the groundwater zone reaches.

Support layers

  • Cantonal groundwater protection (geodienste.ch): blue S1–S3 zones around drinking-water wells and springs, pink protected aquifer sectors (Au). If the red or amber zone touches a blue zone, act first there. Coverage and terms vary by canton.
  • Hydrogeological map 1:100,000 (FOEN): aquifers, water-table contours and flow arrows, on the 8 sheets that exist. Use it to set the direction.
  • Tracer tests (FOEN InfoTracer): where tracer tests were run since 1984, mostly in karst. The layer shows injection points only; the canton holds the results.
  • Click Support layers beside the map to open the panel over the right side of the map; close it with × or Esc. When you switch a layer on, its legend and a link to its source appear under it, and the button shows how many layers are on.
  • Open this place in map.geo.admin.ch opens the federal viewer at the source with these layers.

Things to know

  • The Standard for groundwater menu switches between the drinking-water limit (TBDV) and the AltlV concentration value.
  • Lead and mercury barely move in groundwater, so their zones stay at the source. Arsenic and antimony travel further, fastest in fractured rock.
  • In karst no plume is drawn, because conduits carry water to springs kilometres away within days. Use the tracer-test layer and sample springs.
  • Terrain needs an internet connection to swisstopo. Without it, set the direction yourself.
  • At a location you set, the slope there scales the default erosion. You can overwrite it on the Soil tab.

Where the metal ends up: the mass budget

The result cards give concentrations. The mass budget answers a different question: of every kilogram released, how much sits where after a given time?

  1. Below the result cards, choose the time since the release started: 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 or 100 years.
  2. Choose Floods: none, one extreme flood in year 1, or extreme floods about every 30 or every 10 years (the last stands for more frequent extremes under climate change).
  3. Read the rows. Each is a place: topsoil, washed downslope, floodplain, below the root zone, groundwater (dissolved or held on aquifer sediment), lake water, lake sediment, carried downstream, carried away in air, or other losses. The rows add up to 100%.
  • Bars show the best estimate; the thin line on each bar is the 90% range from 400 runs.
  • The notes below the rows give the travel times used, how floods were counted, and what is not included.
  • The first line of "Where and how to trap it" now names the place holding the most metal, because that is where trapping works best.
  • Flood size, flood delivery to the river and floodplain deposition are assumptions you can change on the Budget & floods tab.

Expect most of a soil release to stay in the topsoil for decades: lead and mercury bind strongly. River releases end up mostly in lake sediment within a year. Floods move far more soil, and its metal, to the river than a normal year does.

What drives the uncertainty

Each range comes from 400 runs in which uncertain inputs vary. This panel shows which of them moves a chosen result most.

  • Pick a result, or a budget share, in the Result menu.
  • Longer bars mean a stronger link. The number is a rank correlation from −1 to +1. "Raises the result" means a higher input value gives a higher result.
  • Measure first names the input to measure to narrow the range most, and how.
  • Enter a measured value (for example a measured Kd) and run again: that input's bar shrinks because its spread is narrower.

Air and the land surface

For releases to air and roads, the platform follows the metal from the stack or road to the ground, and from the ground back to air or water.

  • Size decides distance. The emission is split into gas, fine, coarse and large particles, from the chemical form unless you enter your own shares. Large dust lands within a few hundred metres, fine particles and Hg⁰ travel tens to thousands of kilometres. Rain washes particles out, so wetter sites keep more nearby.
  • Release height and wind. A higher stack spreads the metal further and thinner. Set the prevailing wind on the Air & surface tab to turn the deposition zone on the spread map downwind; the totals stay the same.
  • Most exposed point. Deposition and air concentration are reported where they are highest beyond 100 m from the source, with the nearest NABEL station added as background, and compared with the LRV limits.
  • Forests, snow, sealed ground. Forests catch more fine dust and take up Hg⁰ through their leaves, which reaches the soil with the autumn litter. Snow holds what lands on it until melt; much of the mercury returns to air first. Rain on roofs and roads drains to streams, treatment plants or road shoulders, as you set it.
  • Back to air. Mercury in topsoil slowly returns to air as Hg⁰, and wind lifts a little dust from bare ground.
  • Roads and glaciers. Road traffic (lead only for now) is calculated from traffic, road length and an emission factor; most of it stays within 5 m of the road. Glacier melt releases metal deposited from air in 1950–1990 into the glacier stream.
  • Air quality. PM10 and PM2.5 annual means come from the FOEN maps when you fill inputs from the map. They are context: the dust people already breathe before the release.

Try this: lead, Swiss Plateau preset, release to air, PbO dust, 3,000 kg/yr, release height 15 m, prevailing wind from the southwest. The spread map at 50 years shows topsoil above the VBBo guide value up to about 330 m from the stack, stretching to the northeast.

Lakes and the redox ladder

Two process groups that change which form a metal takes. Both are screening rules, not lake or reactive-transport models.

  • Lake trophic state. More nutrients mean more algae, whose decay uses up oxygen at the lake bottom. With bottom-water oxygen on Estimate from trophic state, a nutrient-poor lake is oxic and a moderately or very nutrient-rich lake has seasonal anoxia. The trophic state also adds to the methylation index.
  • Season. Summer means a stratified lake: seasonal anoxia is active at the bottom and sunlight is strong at the surface. Winter means a mixed, oxic lake. The season changes the methylation index and the forms in lake water. The mass budget always runs the seasonal cycle month by month.
  • Mercury and sunlight. In lake water, sunlight turns dissolved Hg(II) into Hg⁰ gas that escapes to air, and breaks down methylmercury. The default escape is 10% of the lake-water Hg per year, twice as fast from May to September. It appears in the mass budget as "Escaped from lake water as Hg⁰ gas".
  • Redox ladder. Bacteria oxidise organic matter with oxygen first, then nitrate, then manganese and iron oxides, then sulfate; methanogenesis comes last. The ladder sets the electron donors in the water against these electron acceptors and finds the predominant terminal electron-accepting process below the source. Surplus donors reduce the Mn and then the Fe oxides near the source over time; once they are used up, sulfate is reduced. The process reached by the budget year is shown as a result with its electron balance, and CHemTrace uses it as the aquifer redox condition. It is a first indication only: it names the dominant process, it does not calculate species. Species distributions need thermodynamics with measured pH and concentrations; enter your analysis on the Water analysis tab and use the PHREEQC export. Use the ladder when you have data; otherwise keep it off and choose the states on the Soil and Groundwater tabs.
  • Organic contamination as an electron donor. A fuel or oil spill, landfill leachate, sewage or manure adds electron donors that use up oxygen, nitrate and the oxides, and so push the aquifer towards reducing conditions. Dissolving the Fe and Mn oxides makes arsenic and lead more mobile and can also release arsenic naturally present in the sediment, which CHemTrace does not calculate. The organic contaminants count only as electron donors: dissolved hydrocarbons at 0.42 mmol e⁻ per mg, biodegradable COD at 0.125 mmol e⁻ per mg O₂, organic carbon at 4 electrons per C. Their own fate and transformation products are not calculated, because at this level they would be poorly constrained.

Try this: arsenic, Swiss Plateau preset, Redox tab: switch the ladder on, choose Fuel or oil spill and set the dissolved hydrocarbons to 20 mg/L. The predominant process jumps from O₂ reduction to methanogenesis within a year, the aquifer is treated as sulfidic, and the Groundwater tab shows the calculated state greyed out.

Tips, limits and data sources

The biggest gain in accuracy comes from entering a measured Kd or leaching-test result for your soil.

Tips

  • Read % of runs above rather than the single number. It tells you how likely an exceedance is.
  • For arsenic in gardens, set a realistic relative bioavailability (often 30–50%). Otherwise even natural background is flagged by the strict EFSA reference point.
  • The Water regime buttons also change river results in Advanced mode: snowmelt and storm raise discharge and suspended sediment.
  • Use Copy inputs to save a scenario as text, and Paste inputs to load it again.
  • Researchers can rerun any scenario from the browser console with SMP.run("Pb", {…}). The validation page shows how.

Limits

  • Results are screening estimates from simple, documented equations. There is no full chemical speciation. Fast paths to groundwater are flagged in the results and can be tested in the mass budget with the fast-path share.
  • The validation page shows where the model matches Swiss measurements and where it does not.
  • Site data is on a 1 km grid and describes the area, not a parcel.
  • Swiss limit values change. Check the current text on fedlex.admin.ch before using them in a report.
